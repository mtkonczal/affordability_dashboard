Twelve ways to pick a metric

The National tab opens with about 340 pixels of selector — six collapsed category rows and a Select all line — before a single chart. Every metric outside the default seven costs two clicks: expand a row, then find a chip inside it. There is no way to search.

These twelve mockups are live, not screenshots: click them. Each one is measured by the page itself on load, so the height numbers below are real, not asserted. Built for the people who actually use this tab — organizers, state party staff, fact-checkers, reporters, and operatives who arrive knowing the number they need.

Updated after the July review. The team picked options 6, 2 and 11 and ruled out 12, 10, 5 and 3. Those three favorites turn out to be one component in three states, so there is now a merged proposal at the end — and the closing recommendation has been rewritten around it.

What's live now, and what's wrong with it

Three separate problems, often blamed on one

  • Height. Six rows of chrome push the charts below the fold on a laptop. The accordion was added to tame 41 chips, but collapsing rows traded one problem for another: less clutter, more depth.
  • Naming. Big-ticket currently holds childcare, new cars, used cars, car insurance, the ACA benchmark premium, the uninsured rate, rent, home prices, the mortgage rate, and rent burden. That is four different household bills in one drawer. A reporter looking for health insurance will not open “Big-ticket,” and a category nobody opens may as well not exist.
  • No search. With 30 metrics, the fastest possible interface is a text field. Someone who knows they want eggs should type egg and be done. Right now they must guess that eggs live under Groceries and click twice.

Options

Twelve directions, roughly by how much has to change

Ordered by how much has to change, not by preference. Tags say whether the option keeps today's six categories, recuts them into plain language, or drops grouping as the way to navigate. Options eight, nine and ten are the same experiment at three settings — every metric visible, at zero groups, four groups and six.

Option one

Search first

One field, always focused-ready. Type three letters, hit Enter, get the chart. Selected metrics sit beside the field as removable tokens so the current state is readable at a glance. Categories move into the search results as a grey label — still there, no longer a gate.

Option three

Five plain-language tabs

One row, five household-shaped groups, pick one at a time. This is the biggest simplification available: no counts, no chevrons, no multi-select state to reason about. Option eight

Everything, ungrouped

My readFour is the right instinct and this particular four has a flaw I would not ship. Bills, cars & care holds the electric bill, a used car, and a health premium — which is the same sin “Big-ticket” commits today, with a more honest name. At four groups one of them is always the leftovers, so four only works if every group is defined by a principle instead of by what did not fit. The cut I would actually argue for is cash flow: what it costs, what you earn, what you owe, and whether it adds up (rent in hours of work, rent burden, the uninsured rate). No junk drawer — and it surfaces something worth knowing, which is that this tracker carries twenty price series against three income series. For a tool named for affordability, that ratio is the finding, not the filing system.

The merge · six + two + eleven

One list of rows, in two containers

The three favorites are not three designs. Option six is the list of all 30 shown; option two is the same list hidden behind a button; option eleven is what a row of that list should say. Build one row component and two containers for it — a rail on wide screens, a menu below the breakpoint — and all three are satisfied by one piece of code, which is also the mobile fallback the rail needs regardless.

Recommendation, rewritten after the review

Build the row once, then give it two homes

My earlier pick was search plus a headline row plus the table. The team went a different way — and their three choices compose more cleanly than mine did, because 6, 2 and 11 are the same list at three levels of exposure. Nobody has to choose between them. Sequence it so every step ships value on its own and the rail arrives as an enhancement rather than a rewrite:

1

Ship option two properly. The menu, with a filter field and the corrected open state — transparent token, labelled “Hide the list.” This alone deletes the accordion and roughly 340px of chrome, and you need this component for mobile no matter what wins.

2

Put option eleven’s numbers in its rows. Name, change since Dec 2019, plus sign. Percentage points on the rate series, % change only on the index series — the rules already in buildFactText apply to navigation now too.

3

Promote the same rows to a rail above a width breakpoint, with the menu still serving narrow screens and deep scroll. No inner scroll, no border, page background. Test in embed_test.html before it goes near the iframe.

4

Add “See all the numbers” for the full sortable table, defaulting to A–Z. That is the one thing a rail genuinely cannot do, and it keeps sorting opt-in.

Two smaller notes. Ruling out 3, 10 and 12 says something consistent: categories and rankings should not be the navigation. That demotes the taxonomy work from an information-architecture project to a labelling fix — still split Big-ticket, but as group headers inside the rail. And nobody picked search, which I would push back on gently for a deadline audience: it costs nothing here, because it survives as the rail’s filter field rather than its own option.

Notes for whoever builds this

  • Alphabetical ordering is preserved in every option that lists metrics, per the standing comms request. Options eleven and twelve sort by value or change instead, which is a deliberate exception — the sort is the feature — and both default to a stable secondary alphabetical sort within ties.
  • Index series never show a level. Every mockup here shows CPI, groceries, cars, childcare, car insurance, and water & sewer as cumulative % change since Dec 2019, never as index points. Rate series (mortgage, unemployment, delinquency, uninsured, rent burden) change in percentage points.
  • Real numbers, real dates. The values and sparklines in these mockups are read from the committed payload, so nothing here is invented. They are nominal; the Nominal/Real toggle is out of scope for this review.
  • Embed mode. Options six and seven need explicit testing in the ESP iframe — the left rail against the narrower column, the sticky bar against the parent page's header and the height-postMessage script.
  • Keyboard and focus are wired in the search and table mockups, and the modal traps focus and closes on Escape. Whatever ships should hold that floor.
  • Tests to update. Recutting categories means changing CATEGORY_META, CATEGORY_LABELS, and ESP_CATEGORY_COLOR in index.html plus the hardcoded known set in tests/data_contract.test.js, which exists precisely to catch a series stranded in an unknown category.
